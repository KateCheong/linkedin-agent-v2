TOPIC: AI in Finance Services 2026

* AI in financial services is moving from experimentation and individual productivity tools toward embedded AI in business processes.
* GenAI is increasingly being used for knowledge management, document processing, customer operations, software engineering, data analysis and internal operations.
* Agentic AI goes one step further: instead of only generating an answer, an agent can interpret a goal, plan multiple steps, use tools, retrieve information and execute parts of a workflow.
* In financial services, internal operations are one of the most practical starting points for AI because many processes are repetitive, rules-driven, data-heavy and dependent on large amounts of institutional knowledge.
* The interesting question is no longer simply "Can AI do this task?"
* The more useful question is "How should the process be redesigned when AI can perform part of the work?"
* AI adoption in finance is constrained by governance, data quality, cybersecurity, explainability, regulatory expectations, operational resilience and accountability.
* Financial institutions cannot treat AI as just another productivity application. AI becomes part of the operating model when it starts making decisions, accessing systems or executing transactions.
* Human oversight becomes increasingly important as AI moves from answering questions to taking actions.
* A useful distinction:

  * GenAI = generates content or answers.
  * RAG = gives an AI access to controlled enterprise knowledge.
  * AI Agent = reasons/plans and uses tools to accomplish a goal.
  * Agentic workflow = multiple AI/tool steps coordinated toward an outcome.
  * Automation = predefined logic executes predefined steps.
* AI transformation should focus on measurable business outcomes:

  * reduced manual effort
  * reduced operational risk
  * faster turnaround time
  * improved data quality
  * fewer errors
  * better client experience
  * improved scalability
* AI should not be introduced simply because "AI is the trend."
* A strong finance AI use case usually starts with an existing operational pain point and asks whether AI can improve the workflow.

Personal perspective:

* AI is particularly interesting from a Finance Operations perspective because operations sits between business, technology, data and clients.
* Operations professionals understand where the real friction exists: manual reconciliation, emails, spreadsheets, exception handling, unclear ownership, repeated checking and fragmented systems.
* AI can therefore change not only individual tasks but the design of the operating model.
* The future Operations professional may spend less time executing repetitive tasks and more time supervising workflows, handling exceptions, validating decisions and improving processes.

TOPIC: Operations Project Manager Role and Challenges

* Operations Project Management sits at the intersection of:

  * business requirements
  * technology
  * operations
  * client expectations
  * risk and controls
  * regulatory requirements
  * data
  * change management
* A Finance Operations PM often has to translate between business users and technology teams.
* The PM is not necessarily the deepest technical expert, but needs enough technical understanding to challenge assumptions, identify dependencies and explain implications to stakeholders.
* Banking operations projects often involve multiple systems, vendors, internal teams, clients and external counterparties.
* A small change in one operational process can create downstream impacts in:

  * settlement
  * reporting
  * reconciliation
  * accounting
  * client communication
  * regulatory reporting
  * data interfaces
  * controls
* Common PM challenges:

  * unclear requirements
  * changing client expectations
  * dependencies between teams
  * legacy systems
  * multiple sources of truth
  * manual operational workarounds
  * data quality problems
  * limited development capacity
  * testing constraints
  * stakeholder alignment
  * operational readiness
* Traditional project management often assumes requirements can be defined upfront.
* In complex banking transformation, requirements frequently evolve as stakeholders understand the solution better.
* A modern PM therefore needs to manage continuous clarification rather than simply "collect requirements once."
* Strong PM outputs include:

  * project roadmap
  * milestones
  * dependency map
  * RAID log
  * RAG status
  * decision log
  * action tracker
  * stakeholder map
  * testing plan
  * implementation plan
  * operating model / process documentation
  * post-implementation review
* The PM's value is not simply producing status reports.
* The PM creates clarity:

  * What are we trying to achieve?
  * Who owns it?
  * What is blocking it?
  * What decision is required?
  * What happens next?
  * What is the operational impact?
* In AI-enabled projects, PM responsibilities increasingly include understanding AI capability, data dependencies, tool access, guardrails, human oversight and AI risk.

Personal perspective:

* A strong Operations PM should be comfortable moving between a client conversation, a process map, a technical discussion and a senior management status update.
* Banking PMs often become "translators" between business and technology.
* One of the most valuable PM skills is turning ambiguity into an executable plan.

TOPIC: AI Agents vs Traditional Automation in Finance

Traditional automation:

* Executes predefined rules.
* Works best when the process is predictable.
* Usually follows:
  IF condition → THEN action.
* Examples:

  * move a file
  * populate a report
  * send a notification
  * calculate a value
  * trigger a workflow
  * update a system
* Traditional automation is deterministic and relatively easy to test when the inputs and rules are stable.

AI Agent:

* Starts from an objective rather than a fully predefined sequence.
* Can interpret information.
* Can reason over retrieved knowledge.
* Can decide which tool to use.
* Can execute multiple steps.
* Can adapt the workflow based on the information it finds.
* Can escalate to a human when it reaches a boundary.

Useful comparison:

Traditional automation:
"Do these 10 steps."

AI Agent:
"Achieve this outcome using the available tools and rules."

Example in Finance Operations:

Traditional:

* Receive reconciliation file.
* Compare predefined fields.
* Flag differences above threshold.
* Generate exception report.

Agentic:

* Retrieve the latest reconciliation data.
* Compare current exceptions with historical patterns.
* Investigate the likely cause.
* Retrieve relevant operating procedures.
* Check previous resolutions.
* Prepare an explanation.
* Recommend the next action.
* Escalate unusual cases to an Operations analyst.

Important:

* Agentic AI does NOT automatically mean replacing automation.
* The strongest architecture may combine:

  * deterministic automation for predictable tasks
  * AI for interpretation and reasoning
  * APIs/tools for system actions
  * human approval for high-risk decisions

TOPIC: Chatbot as Finance Operations Assistant

* A chatbot is useful when the primary problem is information retrieval and knowledge access.

* Example:
  "What is the procedure for handling this type of collateral dispute?"

* A RAG chatbot can retrieve information from:

  * SOPs
  * project documentation
  * policy documents
  * product documentation
  * historical project materials
  * FAQs

* The chatbot becomes more powerful when connected to operational systems.

* Example:
  "Why is this margin call still unresolved?"

* The AI could retrieve:

  * current exposure
  * collateral balance
  * dispute status
  * agreement information
  * relevant procedures

* However, answering is different from acting.

* A chatbot can explain the issue.

* An agent can potentially investigate the issue and execute approved actions.

* This creates a progression:

  Search → RAG chatbot → AI assistant → Tool-using agent → Agentic workflow

* The risk also increases along this progression because the system gains more access and autonomy.

TOPIC: Human Role in AI-Assisted Operations

* AI does not remove the need for humans in Finance Operations.
* It changes where human effort is applied.
* Human work moves from repetitive execution toward:

  * judgement
  * exception handling
  * validation
  * governance
  * process design
  * stakeholder management
  * escalation
  * continuous improvement

Human Layer:

1. System Prompt

* Human defines the agent's role, behaviour, objectives, rules and boundaries.
* Defines what the agent should and should not do.

2. Knowledge / RAG

* Human decides what information the agent is allowed to use.
* Enterprise knowledge should be controlled and relevant.
* Retrieval quality directly affects answer quality.

3. Tool Design

* Human decides what tools the agent can access.
* Examples:

  * search database
  * retrieve report
  * query API
  * create ticket
  * send notification
  * update system
* Tool access is effectively part of the agent's authority.

4. Guardrails

* Human defines boundaries.
* Examples:

  * maximum transaction amount
  * approved systems
  * restricted data
  * mandatory human approval
  * confidence thresholds
  * prohibited actions
  * escalation rules

5. Human-in-the-loop

* Human approves actions before execution.
* Appropriate for higher-risk financial operations.

6. Human-on-the-loop

* Human supervises an agent that can operate more independently.
* Human intervenes when the agent reaches an exception or predefined boundary.

7. Monitoring

* Human monitors:

  * accuracy
  * hallucination
  * tool usage
  * failure rate
  * exception rate
  * operational impact
  * model drift
  * unexpected behaviour

Key idea:

* The human is not simply the "user" of the AI.
* The human designs the environment in which the AI operates.

TOPIC: RAG in Finance Operations

RAG = Retrieval-Augmented Generation.

* RAG connects an AI model with an external knowledge base.
* Instead of relying only on the model's training knowledge, the system retrieves relevant enterprise information before generating an answer.

Typical Finance Operations RAG architecture:

Documents
→ chunking
→ embeddings
→ vector database / FAISS
→ similarity search
→ relevant context
→ LLM
→ answer

Possible knowledge sources:

* SOPs
* project documents
* process maps
* product documentation
* client documentation
* policy documents
* historical issues
* FAQs
* meeting notes
* approved procedures

Why RAG is valuable in banking:

* Enterprise knowledge is fragmented.
* Procedures change.
* Employees may not know where the latest document is.
* Much operational knowledge exists in people's heads or historical emails.
* RAG can make institutional knowledge easier to access.

Important limitations:

* Bad source documents create bad answers.
* Poor chunking can lose context.
* Incorrect retrieval produces incorrect answers.
* Sensitive information requires access control.
* RAG does not automatically guarantee factual accuracy.

TOPIC: AI Agent Design Principles

An AI agent should be designed around an objective and operating boundary.

Core components:

* Goal
* Instructions
* Knowledge
* Memory
* Reasoning / decision logic
* Tools
* Guardrails
* Human escalation
* Monitoring

A useful design question:

"What is the smallest amount of autonomy required to solve the problem?"

Do not give an agent unnecessary tools.

More tools = more capability but also:

* more complexity
* more failure modes
* greater security risk
* more difficult testing
* more difficult governance

Tool design should therefore be treated as part of operational control.

Example:

Agent can:

* read exposure data
* retrieve agreement terms
* search SOP
* calculate difference
* draft an explanation

Agent cannot:

* release collateral
* change client instructions
* approve a financial movement

Human approval is required before high-impact actions.

TOPIC: AI and Process Re-Design

* AI should not simply be placed on top of an inefficient process.
* First understand the current process.
* Identify:

  * manual steps
  * duplicate work
  * unnecessary approvals
  * handoffs
  * waiting time
  * exception points
  * data quality issues
  * system limitations
* Then determine which steps should be:

  * removed
  * automated
  * AI-assisted
  * redesigned
  * retained as human judgement

A useful transformation model:

Current Process
→ Process Simplification
→ Traditional Automation
→ AI Assistance
→ Agentic Workflow
→ Human Exception Management

The biggest opportunity may not be "AI replacing a person."
It may be removing unnecessary steps from the process so the person can focus on higher-value work.

TOPIC: Operations Efficiency and FTE Reduction

* FTE reduction is often discussed as the obvious benefit of automation.
* A more useful framing is capacity creation.
* Automation can allow existing teams to absorb more volume without proportional headcount growth.
* Potential benefits:

  * lower operating cost
  * faster turnaround
  * reduced repetitive work
  * reduced operational errors
  * higher scalability
  * improved employee experience
* However, reducing FTE without redesigning the process can simply move work somewhere else.
* Good transformation measures:

  * volume per FTE
  * processing time
  * error rate
  * exception rate
  * cost per transaction
  * SLA performance
  * automation rate
  * STP rate
  * manual touchpoints

Personal perspective:

* The interesting PM question is not "How many people can AI replace?"
* It is "How much operational capacity can we create, and what should people do with that capacity?"

TOPIC: Key Finance and Operations Terminology

* STP: Straight-Through Processing — automated end-to-end transaction processing with minimal or no manual intervention.
* Middle Office: Function that monitors positions, exposures and risks and manages dealing, settlement systems and controls.
* Back Office: Functions supporting transaction processing, settlement, reconciliation, accounting and operational controls.
* Front Office: Client-facing and trading/investment activities.
* Reconciliation: Comparing records from different systems or parties to identify differences.
* Exception Management: Investigating and resolving transactions or records that do not follow the expected process.
* Margin Call: Request for additional collateral when exposure or margin requirements create a shortfall.
* Collateral: Assets provided to secure financial obligations.
* Exposure: Financial amount at risk between counterparties.
* Net Exposure: Exposure after considering collateral and other agreed adjustments.
* CSA: Credit Support Annex governing collateral arrangements between counterparties.
* MTA: Minimum Transfer Amount — minimum amount required before a collateral transfer is made.
* Haircut: Reduction applied to the market value of collateral for risk-management purposes.
* Settlement: Process of completing the transfer of cash or securities after a transaction.
* SWIFT: Global messaging network used by financial institutions.
* MT: Legacy SWIFT message format.
* MX: ISO 20022-based XML message format.
* ISO 20022: International standard providing a common framework for structured financial messages and data.
* API: Application Programming Interface enabling systems to communicate programmatically.
* RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Generation.
* LLM: Large Language Model.
* GenAI: Generative AI.
* Agentic AI: AI systems capable of planning and executing multi-step tasks with varying levels of autonomy.
* Human-in-the-loop: Human approval or intervention is embedded into the workflow.
* Human-on-the-loop: Human supervises an autonomous system and intervenes when necessary.
* RAID: Risks, Assumptions, Issues and Dependencies.
* RAG Status: Red, Amber, Green project health indicator.
* UAT: User Acceptance Testing.
* FRD: Functional Requirements Document.
* STP Rate: Percentage of transactions completed without manual intervention.
* DORA: Digital Operational Resilience Act, the EU framework for ICT and operational resilience in financial entities.

TOPIC: Collateral Management as a Service

* Collateral Management is a critical part of financial market operations.
* It involves managing collateral exchanged between counterparties to mitigate credit and counterparty risk.
* Collateral management can involve:

  * exposure calculation
  * margin calls
  * collateral allocation
  * collateral movements
  * substitutions
  * settlement
  * reconciliation
  * dispute management
  * reporting
* It sits strongly within the Middle Office / post-trade ecosystem.
* Collateral processes can involve multiple counterparties, custodians, internal systems, market infrastructures and service providers.
* Operational complexity comes from:

  * different agreements
  * different currencies
  * different collateral types
  * different settlement instructions
  * different counterparties
  * different thresholds
  * different system interfaces
  * exceptions and disputes

Collateral Management as a Service:

* Technology providers can provide collateral management capabilities to financial institutions rather than every institution building the entire platform internally.
* This can support standardisation and scalability.
* APIs can enable collateral platforms to integrate with surrounding banking systems.
* A service-oriented architecture can separate:

  * collateral calculation
  * settlement
  * messaging
  * reporting
  * downstream processing

Personal perspective:

* Collateral Management is a good example of why financial operations cannot be understood only from the business side or technology side.
* A seemingly simple "move collateral" process involves agreements, exposure, calculations, settlement instructions, messaging, systems, controls and human exceptions.
* This makes collateral management a useful real-world example for explaining digital transformation in Finance Operations.

TOPIC: ISO 20022 and Financial Messaging Transformation

* ISO 20022 provides a common framework for structured financial messaging.
* Compared with legacy message formats, ISO 20022 supports richer and more granular structured data.
* Migration is not simply a message-format conversion.
* It can require changes to:

  * data mapping
  * interfaces
  * validation
  * account information
  * operational processes
  * testing
  * downstream systems
  * reporting
* A key transformation challenge is preserving business meaning when moving between legacy and new message formats.
* Teams need to understand both:

  * the technical message structure
  * the underlying business process

Personal perspective:

* ISO 20022 is a good example of a transformation where "technology change" actually becomes "operational change."
* Changing the message standard can affect multiple teams even when the business process itself appears unchanged.

TOPIC: API-First Operations

* APIs allow systems to exchange structured information directly.
* API-based integration can reduce dependency on manual file exchanges and email-based workflows.
* In Finance Operations, APIs can support:

  * transaction processing
  * settlement
  * reporting
  * reconciliation
  * client data exchange
  * workflow orchestration
* API migration should be treated as a business transformation, not only a technical integration.
* Important PM questions:

  * What is the source of truth?
  * What data is sent?
  * What data is returned?
  * What happens when the API fails?
  * How are errors handled?
  * What is the fallback process?
  * Who owns the interface?
  * How is the output validated?
* An API can automate data movement, but it does not automatically solve a poorly designed process.

TOPIC: Client Change Management in Financial Services

* Financial services projects often involve clients, internal teams and external technology providers.
* Client expectations can evolve during delivery.
* The PM needs to balance:

  * client relationship
  * scope
  * timeline
  * technical feasibility
  * operational risk
  * internal capacity
* Effective client communication is not only reporting status.
* It involves helping the client understand:

  * what is changing
  * why it matters
  * what they need to do
  * what decisions are required
  * what the timeline means
  * what risks exist
* In modern delivery, collaboration can become more iterative and relationship-driven rather than relying entirely on a large upfront requirements document.
* However, important decisions and scope boundaries still need to be documented.
* "Less bureaucracy" does not mean "less control."

TOPIC: Project Delivery in the AI Era

* Traditional project delivery often assumes:
  Requirements → Design → Build → Test → Deploy.
* AI projects are more iterative:
  Problem → Experiment → Prototype → Evaluate → Redesign → Pilot → Measure → Scale.
* AI capability changes rapidly, so the solution may evolve during the project.
* PMs need to manage uncertainty rather than pretending everything is known upfront.
* Important AI project outputs can include:

  * use-case definition
  * process map
  * AI capability assessment
  * data assessment
  * architecture
  * RAG knowledge structure
  * tool inventory
  * guardrail design
  * evaluation criteria
  * human escalation model
  * pilot results
  * benefit measurement
  * production readiness assessment
* Success criteria should be measurable.
* Example:
  "AI assistant implemented" is not a business outcome.
* Better:
  "Reduce average investigation time by 40% while maintaining an acceptable accuracy threshold."

TOPIC: AI Governance and Operational Risk

* Financial institutions need governance because AI can introduce operational, cyber, compliance and model risks.
* Key questions:

  * Who owns the AI?
  * What data can it access?
  * What decisions can it make?
  * What tools can it use?
  * How are outputs validated?
  * How are failures detected?
  * Can actions be audited?
  * Can the system be stopped?
  * What happens if the model changes?
* AI governance should be designed into the workflow rather than added after deployment.
* Agentic AI increases the importance of:

  * access control
  * auditability
  * tool permissions
  * human escalation
  * monitoring
  * testing
  * incident management
* In Finance Operations, operational resilience matters because a failure in an automated process can affect clients, transactions and financial markets.

TOPIC: AI + Institutional Knowledge

* One of the biggest hidden assets in Operations is institutional knowledge.
* Experienced employees know:

  * why a process exists
  * which exceptions matter
  * which client behaviours are unusual
  * which workaround is safe
  * which system is actually the source of truth
  * which historical issue caused a similar problem
* Much of this knowledge is not captured in formal documentation.
* AI creates an opportunity to capture and make this knowledge searchable.
* RAG can expose documented knowledge.
* Agent workflows can potentially combine documented knowledge with operational data.
* The challenge is ensuring that the AI learns from approved knowledge rather than random historical information.
* The future advantage may therefore not simply be "having an LLM."
* It may be having a well-structured organisational knowledge layer.

TOPIC: AI Catalyst Mindset

* AI Catalyst = someone who helps an organisation move from AI curiosity to practical adoption.
* The role is not necessarily to build the AI model.
* The role can be:

  * identify useful problems
  * connect business pain points with AI capabilities
  * prototype solutions
  * explain AI to non-technical stakeholders
  * challenge unrealistic expectations
  * design workflows
  * identify risks
  * measure value
* AI adoption often fails because organisations start with the technology rather than the problem.
* A useful AI Catalyst asks:
  "What problem are we solving?"
  "Why is AI appropriate?"
  "What should remain human?"
  "What data does the AI need?"
  "What tools does it need?"
  "What could go wrong?"
  "How do we measure success?"
